Haworth

Brontë Country

← Back: Home

Haworth village was the centre of the Brontë sisters’ lives. An insular, remote settlement in the Pennines, it is surrounded by wild, uncompromising moors. This landscape provided much of the backdrop for the sisters’ writing, and a home to look back on as they made their way in the wider world.

The Old School Room

Located between St Michael & All Angels Church and the Brontë Parsonage Museum, the Old School Room has served the people of Haworth for almost 180 years. It was first built through a grant procured by Patrick Brontë in 1832. All three Brontë sisters educated the poor children of Haworth there, and it is the only place where they were all employed. Charlotte Brontë’s wedding reception was also held there, attended by 500 guests. Patrick Brontë later secured a further grant to enable the school to open on weekdays as well as function as a Sunday school, and Charlotte’s husband, Arthur Bell Nicholls, secured funding for an extension to the building in 1850. The school was replaced in 1903, but it remains a central part of Brontë lore and tourism in the area. Over the years it has functioned as a gymnasium, library, youth hostel, and can even be hired out for events today.

St Michaels and All Angels Church

Patrick Brontë spent 41 years as the vicar of Haworth Parish Church, baptising around 240 children a year. Tragically, these children had an average life expectancy of just 22 years, with 40 per cent not living beyond their sixth birthday. Ironically, the overcrowding of the church graveyard, along with broader sanitation issues, contributed to many of these deaths as the local water supply became contaminated. All of the Brontë family are buried at the church except for Anne, who is buried in Scarborough, North Yorkshire. Anne and Charlotte were regular attendees at their father’s services, but the reclusive Emily is believed to have attended far less frequently. This likely added to her enigma among locals at the time, just as her legacy remains mysterious to readers and scholars today. Brontë Waterfall

Brontë Waterfall on South Dean Beck is surrounded mainly by moorland and farmland near Haworth. It was a favourite spot of the three Brontë sisters, who often walked there with their half-mastiff dog, Keeper. The walk to the waterfall is said to have inspired parts of Wuthering Heights.

Top Withens

The location of Top Withen, rather than the ruin itself, is said to have inspired the setting of the farmhouse in Wuthering Heights. It was already a ruin in Emily Brontë’s time, and the word “Withens” comes from a Yorkshire term for willows. When the house was inhabited by a farming family, it was described as one of the windiest places in all of Yorkshire. Completely exposed, it was supposedly battered by winds so fierce they could shake the rafters of the farmhouse's roof.

Haworth Parsonage Museum

The Brontë Parsonage Museum is maintained by the Brontë Society, one of the oldest literary societies in the English-speaking world. After the deaths of the Brontës, many servants and friends sold keepsakes relating to the family. The society was formed to reclaim these items and preserve the parsonage for posterity.
