Overview

The Lives, Landscape, and Legacy of the Brontës

Meet the sisters →

The Brontë sisters lived in Haworth Parsonage in the Pennines in West Yorkshire. Their mother, Maria Branwell, died when all her children were still very young, followed shortly afterward by two less well known daughters, Maria and Elizabeth. Their father, Patrick Brontë, a curate, had originally moved there from County Down, Ireland, which accounts for the sisters’ Northern Irish accents. His only son, Branwell, struggled with alcohol and opium addiction. Some critics suggest inspired Anne’s novel The Tenant of Wildfell Hall.

The early Brontë years

In the early 1840s, Charlotte and Emily Brontë aspired to open a school, encouraged in part by Anne’s success working as a governess. To strengthen their qualifications, Charlotte and Emily travelled to Brussels to study and work as tutors, since all respectable schools of the time taught French. However, when their aunt Elizabeth Branwell died—who had been a surrogate mother to the children—they returned home. Emily, already terribly homesick, stayed in Haworth when Charlotte later returned to Brussels alone for a second period of study. Ultimately, despite their training, Haworth’s remoteness made it an unattractive prospect for potential pupils, and the sisters’ school plans quietly faded, giving way to their literary ambitions.

Haworth and the Moors

The isolated and bleak backdrop of Haworth can be seen in the remote settings of all three sisters’ works. Freedom to explore the wild, churning moors that surrounded them led to an intense individuality and introspection that can be seen in the heroines of their most famous works. A dutiful existence serving their father in the parsonage contrasted strongly with the endless moorland around them. This resonated in their writing through a constant tension in their plotlines between duty and the desire for freedom and self-determination. The close-knit, parochial village of Haworth and its inhabitants drove all three sisters to turn to psychological examination and interiority and social injustices as fuel for their storytelling.
